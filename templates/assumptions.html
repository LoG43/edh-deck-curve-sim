How This Simulator Thinks

Every heuristic, simplifying assumption, and behavioral decision that shapes the numbers you see.

The simulator is not an exact rules engine — it plays out thousands of simplified Commander games and tracks whether each nonland card's cost was payable turn by turn. To make that tractable, it makes a fixed set of deliberate calls about how a "typical" game plays out. None of these are bugs; they're documented design decisions. This page explains each one in plain language: what the tool decided, why it decided that, and what it actually changes in your results.

1

Mana rocks work the turn you play them

Decision
A mana rock (a nonland, noncreature artifact that taps for mana, like Sol Ring or Arcane Signet) is treated as usable for mana the same turn it's cast.
Why
This matches the actual Magic rules — summoning sickness only restricts creatures, not artifacts, so a freshly-cast rock can tap for mana immediately in real games.
Impact
Decks leaning on artifact ramp will show stronger, faster mana availability than decks of the same land count without rocks. This is accurate, not an inflated number.
2

Mana dorks need a turn to "wake up"

Decision
A mana dork (a creature that taps for mana, like Llanowar Elves) can't be tapped for mana until the turn after it's cast, unless its own oracle text says "Haste."
Why
This is the real rule for summoning sickness on creatures.
Impact
Two accelerants that both "add one mana" won't look equally fast in the results if one is a rock and one is a dork — the dork's mana shows up a turn later. This is intentional and mirrors a real game.
3

Ramp spells fetch a tapped land by default

Decision
Spells that search your library for a land and put it onto the battlefield (Rampant Growth, Cultivate, Nature's Lore) are assumed to put that land into play tapped, so it's usable starting the following turn.
Why
Most budget and midrange ramp spells do put the land into play tapped — it's the common case across the format, even though a handful of premium effects (Skyshroud Claim, Farseek into a dual) don't.
Impact
If your list leans heavily on the untapped-ramp exceptions, this assumption will slightly undersell your deck's actual speed — a conservative error, not an inflated one. There's a toggle (ASSUME_RAMP_LANDS_TAPPED) near the top of the script for lists like that.
4

One accelerant per turn, but every card is checked independently

Decision
The simulated player deploys at most one ramp spell or mana rock/dork per turn (the cheapest affordable one) — mirroring "play a land, play one accelerant" sequencing. Separately, every nonland card in the whole decklist is checked each turn for whether its cost could have been paid with whatever mana was actually available that turn, regardless of whether that specific card was drawn that game.
Why
This answers the manabase question ("does my mana base support this cost by this turn") rather than trying to simulate one exact, specific play-by-play game line for every card, which would require guessing what the simulated player "chose" to cast among cards it never actually drew.
Impact
Read every percentage as "how reliably could my mana produce this," not "how often did I actually cast this card in a real game." That's the right lens for curve and manabase tuning, which is this tool's whole purpose — but it's not a substitute for full gameplay simulation.
5

Colored mana is matched with a "most-constrained-source-first" solver

Decision
When checking whether a card's colored pips can be paid, the tool hands out mana starting with the sources that produce the fewest colors (i.e. the least flexible ones) first, saving flexible sources for whatever's left.
Why
This is a fast, good real-world approximation of how a careful player sequences mana — it's not a full constraint solver, because an exhaustive solver would be dramatically slower across tens of thousands of simulated games for a benefit that rarely changes the outcome.
Impact
For the vast majority of manabases this matches optimal play exactly. Extremely convoluted manabases (lots of cards that each produce several different colors in unusual combinations) could in rare edge cases be mismodeled at the margins — but this hasn't been observed to matter in any of the decklists tested so far.
6

Land drops (and accelerant picks) target whatever's cheapest and uncastable

Decision
Each turn's land drop is chosen — when there's a choice among lands in hand — to best supply the colors needed by the cheapest nonland card that can't yet be cast. Mana rocks, mana dorks, ramp spells, and ordinary action spells all compete for this "cheapest uncastable" slot on equal footing, purely by mana value.
Why
Earlier versions of this tool only counted "action" spells when deciding what a land drop should aim for, which meant a turn-two mana rock's own color needs were invisible to the land- drop logic — an unrealistic gap, since a real player absolutely sequences lands around casting their accelerants too.
Impact
Decks with colored-mana-hungry rocks or dorks (e.g. a green dork that costs G in a three-color deck) will show more realistic, usually improved, early-game numbers versus older versions of this tool. This is the most recently changed piece of decision logic in the simulator.
7

Your commander is free — it doesn't compete with your draws

Decision
A card placed under a Commander heading in your decklist file is cast from the command zone and removed from the simulated 99/100-card library entirely — it will never be drawn, and never occupies a card slot.
Why
This is how the format actually works: your commander starts in the command zone, not your library.
Impact
If you forget the Commander heading, that card is instead treated as an ordinary 99th/100th library card — it can be drawn, milled, or stuck in your opening hand like anything else, and its own curve numbers will appear in the results table instead of being excluded. It does not get "better" numbers either way — marking it correctly just removes it from the table and from the shared library, matching a real game.
8

Double-faced cards are modeled as a single face — and land faces win

Decision
For modal double-faced cards (MDFCs), transforming cards, and similar two-face cards: if either face is a land, the whole card is modeled as only that land — its spell-side face is ignored for curve purposes. Otherwise, the front (spell) face is used.
Why
A card like an MDFC land//spell can only ever be one or the other in a given game, and for a manabase/curve analysis, treating it as a flexible extra land is the more useful and more conservative framing — it deliberately doesn't credit you with "always having access to both halves."
Impact
MDFC lands (e.g. Sea Gate Restoration // Sea Gate, Reborn) count toward your land total and colors, and their castable-spell-side percentages won't appear at all in the results table, because the tool never evaluates that side. If you specifically want to analyze the spell side of an MDFC, this tool will undercount it — that's a known, deliberate scope limit.
9

Fetchlands are fully played out, including real deck-thinning

Decision
A fetchland (e.g. Arid Mesa, Prismatic Vista) is sacrificed the same turn it's played, a matching land is searched for and actually removed from that game's simulated library, and the found land's own colors and tapped-or-not status are used — not the fetchland's. If no legal target remains, the fetch still resolves and finds nothing, same as in a real game. Fetch targets are chosen to favor whatever color the cheapest uncastable spell in hand currently needs.
Why
Fetchlands genuinely thin your deck (later draws are very slightly more likely to be spells) and genuinely convert into whatever land you actually chose — modeling this loosely would misrepresent both the deck-thinning value and the color-fixing value fetches are played for.
Impact
Decks with fetchlands will show slightly better long-game color consistency than a naive land count would suggest, and this compounds correctly with any tapped/shock lands the fetch can find. Life-total cost of shockland fetches (paying 2 life) is not tracked — see the "out of scope" list below.
10

Enters-tapped lands are checked against your actual battlefield

Decision
Any land that can enter tapped conditionally — shocklands, and the slow/fast/bond/check/battle cycles — has that condition checked against what's really on the battlefield in that simulated game at the moment it enters, whether it was drawn normally or found by a fetch.
Why
Whether a check land (needs a matching basic type) or a battle land (needs two-or-more other lands) enters tapped is genuinely conditional on board state — treating them all as "always tapped" or "always untapped" would misrepresent both the good and bad cases.
Impact
These lands' contribution to your mana will vary game-to-game based on what else was drawn and played, which is realistic — you may notice their effective reliability sits between a true tapland and a true untapped land, which is the expected, correct behavior for these card types.
11

Scope: this is a manabase tool, not a full gameplay simulator

Decision
Two categories of effects are deliberately not modeled at all, as a scope choice rather than an oversight:
  • Life totals. Paying 2 life for a shockland, or losing life to a fetchland's activation cost on certain lands, is never tracked. The mana is assumed to simply happen. This would only matter for burn-matchup or life-total-sensitive analysis, which this tool doesn't attempt.
  • Nonland "enters tapped" or fetch-like effects. A creature that enters tapped, or a spell that sacrifices something to tutor a nonland permanent onto the battlefield, is not specially modeled — only lands get the fetch/tapped-land treatment. Mana rocks and mana dorks keep their own separate same-turn/next-turn rules (see items 1 and 2 above) and are unaffected by this.
Impact
If your question is "will my manabase produce the colors and quantity of mana my curve needs, by which turn" — this tool answers it directly. If your question involves life totals or nonland board-state tutoring effects, this tool's numbers simply don't speak to that; treat it as a manabase-and-curve-only reliability report.
12

Exactly one card is drawn per turn — no card draw spells

Decision
The simulated player draws exactly one card every turn, including turn 1, and nothing else. Card-draw spells and effects — Sign in Blood, Faithless Looting, wheel effects, Rhystic Study triggers, extra land drops, and so on — are not modeled at all. The simulated player never sees more cards than the plain one-per-turn baseline, no matter what's actually in your decklist.
Why
Modeling card draw and card-advantage engines accurately would require simulating what the player actually casts and when — a much larger, more speculative undertaking than answering "is the manabase itself sound." Assuming the plain baseline keeps the tool honestly scoped to mana availability instead of guessing at a play pattern for every possible draw engine.
Impact
This is one of the most significant sources of pessimism in the whole tool. If your list runs card draw or extra-card-selection effects, your real games will find lands and key spells meaningfully faster than these numbers suggest, because seeing more cards raises the odds of having the right land or accelerant in hand by any given turn. There's no toggle for this — treat every percentage on this site as a floor on a deck with real card advantage, not a ceiling.
